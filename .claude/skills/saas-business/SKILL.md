# SaaS Business

## Value metric

Pick the one unit that grows as the customer gets more value, and price on it. For an operations platform this is usually throughput (orders, drops, seats), never "features". A wrong value metric caps revenue permanently; changing it later is a repricing crisis.

## Tiering

Three tiers. The middle one is the one you want sold, so it must be the obvious choice — the cheap tier exists to make it look reasonable, the expensive tier to make it look affordable. Differentiate tiers by *scale and depth*, never by withholding correctness or safety. Compliance features must be in the entry tier; charging extra to obey the law is both unsellable and indefensible.

## Moat, in ascending order of durability

1. Features — copied in a quarter.
2. Integrations — copied in a year.
3. **Embedded regulatory logic** — a competitor must acquire the same jurisdictional expertise and keep it current. Durable and narrow.
4. **Data network effects** — every customer's operations improve the forecasts every other customer receives. Compounds and cannot be bought.

Build for 3 and 4 deliberately.

## Vertical software advantage

A generic platform must beat incumbents on everything. A vertical platform beats them on the ten things that specific operator does nightly. Narrow deeply: "operations platform for night delivery in Switzerland" outsells "delivery platform" at ten times the price, because the buyer sees their own workflow reflected back.

## Go-to-market for small operators

- Sell the outcome in their words ("your drops are profitable, your night shifts are legal"), never the architecture.
- Time-to-first-value under one hour or they never return.
- Land on the painful module, expand into the platform.
- Migration in, not out: import their existing catalogue on day one.
